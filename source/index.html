Seventy-two hours.

That is roughly the window in which people trapped by a collapse are still found alive. Inside, GPS doesn't reach, radio carries a few rooms at most, and one drone can only search one room at a time.

More drones don't fix that on their own. If none of them knows what the others have already searched, ten drones search the same rooms over and over.

One drone asks one question: where next?

It draws its own map as it flies. LiDAR marks each patch of floor as free, occupied or still unknown. Where free meets unknown is a frontier, and the drone flies to the one that reveals the most for the least travel.

Cut its radio and it simply carries on alone. The swarm is added on top of a complete drone, never needed to hold one up.

No leader. No pilot. No orders.

Each drone shares only what it has searched, about half a kilobyte a second, and the maps merge cleanly even when a message arrives late or never. Nobody assigns rooms: a drone just steers clear of the frontiers its neighbours have already claimed.

The fleet is held at the edge between flocking and scattering, where one drone's turn ripples through the rest without a command being sent. That threshold is where the name comes from.

Floor area flown until nothing is left to search. Hatched: rooms searched again by a drone that didn't know.

Finished is a number, not a feeling.

Every pocket nobody has seen yet is counted and numbered, wherever it hides. At first the count climbs, as the drones find rooms nobody knew were there; then it falls as each one is searched. A pocket keeps its number until it is gone. The search isn't over until the count reaches zero.

Only then does the swarm stop and hand the responders a report of everything it found.

The map is the means. Finding people is the point.

Back in the void, at first light. Thermal sees the warmth through the rubble; the camera confirms the shape. A person is marked only when both agree, and the responders outside get the spot and the way in.
